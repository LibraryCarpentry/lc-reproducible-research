# Reproducible Research Workflows: pedagogical validation

Reviewed 2026-09-16 against the supplied validation request and current working-tree lesson files. Locations below refer to those files before any further edits. This is a content and teaching review using the project's supplied CLDT principles, not a fresh mechanical validation or an official lifecycle determination. Lesson sources have not been edited.

The revision is substantially stronger, but I would keep it in alpha for one focused correction pass and a timed pilot. The case is useful, the library-role reframe largely works, and the citation framing is improved. Remaining blockers are incomplete assessment alignment, an exercise budget that cannot accommodate the listed activities, and a few explanations that teach conflicting concepts.

**1. Objective-to-assessment mapping**

An assessment counts when learners must demonstrate the objective. An explanation, a tool list, or an answer supplied by the instructor does not by itself count. “Partial” means a relevant activity exists but does not elicit the whole stated performance.

**2. Timing realism**

Episode 2's 46 minutes is not supported by its current activity budget. The labeled activities are 5 minutes for classification (line 82), 5 for disciplinary examples (102), 5 for terminology discussion (121), and 10 for the crisis discussion (188): **25 minutes**, already exceeding `exercises: 24`. The first classification challenge (45) and the Cool Access LA challenge (150) are untimed. Allowing 2 and 5 minutes respectively makes 32 exercise minutes. With 22 teaching minutes, that is **54 minutes before contingency**. Instructions, reporting back and checking answers must be included within those activity allocations.

My planning estimate is **55–60 minutes** for the present scope with firm facilitation, not a measured runtime. The pilot's 50 minutes is useful evidence but not a precise calibration for a revised version. For the next pilot, use `teaching: 23, exercises: 32`, totaling 55, and reserve five minutes of schedule slack. Keep the linked articles as optional reading; opening and reading them during the lesson would need more time. If 46 minutes is fixed, remove the first redundant classification challenge and the broad “additional examples” discussion, then retime the remaining activities. Do not compress every discussion to make the arithmetic work.

Episode 4's **42 minutes is possible as a tightly facilitated orientation**, but fragile for a cold instructor and novice audience. Its 15 exercise minutes must cover stage sorting, the codebook choice, opening and interpreting the README template, discussing qualitative alternatives, and debriefs. A plausible current allocation is 3 + 2 + 6 + 5 = **16 minutes**, with no browser delay or separate exit check. There is no walkthrough of an actual artifact in the current teaching budget.

Keep the agreed tool list. For a revised pilot, use **30 teaching + 20 exercises = 50 minutes**. Allocate exercises as 3 minutes stage sorting, 4 minutes documentation/version-control choices, 6 minutes README work, 5 minutes qualitative traceability, and 2 minutes the four-tool exit check, including debriefs. The 30 teaching minutes must include one concrete worked artifact rather than live demonstrations of every product. Supply a small local excerpt of the README template so browsing is optional. If 42 minutes is fixed, publish an explicit delivery script and test it before claiming that it fits.

All five currently declared episode totals (23, 46, 20, 42, 23) fall within the project's 20–60-minute guideline. That does not establish realism. The whole lesson currently totals **154 minutes before breaks**; the proposed 55- and 50-minute budgets increase this to 171 minutes.

The pilot's “missing discussion timings” issue is only partly resolved. Missing estimates remain in Episode 3's two discussions (lines 41 and 69), Episode 4's two-part discussion (120), and Episode 5's reflection (53). Assign Episode 3 five minutes to each revised discussion, including share-back; assign Episode 5 four minutes to its challenge and four to reflection. The untimed challenges in Episodes 1, 2, 4 and 5 also need allocations in the instructor run sheet. Episode 1 can use five minutes for the replacement openness challenge and three for its existing discussion.

**3. Does Cool Access LA do cognitive work?**

Yes. Episode 2 makes learners separate access restrictions from reproducibility. Episode 4's cryptic-column problem makes them diagnose a documentation gap and choose a codebook. Episode 3 connects a barrier to support; Episode 5 connects a request to a service. Those are purposeful uses of the agreed light-spine structure.

Three execution problems remain:

- **Overview, lines 153 and 161:** the different-city scenario is labeled only replication, whereas the preceding exercise associates a different context or population with generalization. The categories need not be mutually exclusive. Replace the answer with: “This is replication using new data. Because the setting changes, it can also contribute evidence about generalizability; one matching result does not establish that the finding holds everywhere.” Alternatively, use another sample from the same target population if the exercise needs one unambiguous label. The prompt must also say the analysis method is retained; the same questions alone do not establish that.
- **Overview, lines 154–162:** the question asks about the whole project's reproducibility, but the solution establishes only that the survey component could be reproducible. Sharing data and code alone does not demonstrate a successful rerun. State: “The survey analysis can be reproduced if the documented workflow runs successfully. Authorized reviewers can trace the interview analysis using its coding records and decision notes. Public access and reproducibility are separate questions.” Do not certify the entire mixed-methods project from the survey example.
- **Tools, lines 96–104:** this is a useful tool-to-purpose summary, but not yet a worked example. Seven or more names are not inherently too many; the missing element is a visible problem, decision, artifact and check. Keep the bullets and add one small fictional artifact: `q1a = days in the past 7 days when the respondent could not reach a cooling space; allowed values 0–7; 99 = no answer`. Explain why these details belong in a codebook, then ask why treating 99 as a count would distort results. State that this is invented teaching data. Let the later version-control challenge provide the next decision rather than adding seven demonstrations.

The final Quarto bullet should say that it can regenerate computational results and assemble them with documented qualitative interpretations. Combining prose and results in one report does not automatically reproduce the reasoning behind interview findings. Likewise, change “so the analysis still runs the same way next year” in the renv bullet to “helps recreate the package environment for a later rerun.” This retains the already-corrected distinction between environment management and containers.

**5. Library-role reframe**

The added paragraph at line 29, the research-team responsibility at line 34, and the staffing/partnership reflection at line 57 substantially resolve the pilot's core objection. They are clear statements of scope, not mere hedging.

The remaining problem is the universal low-to-high-effort ordering. “Offering training” can require substantial expertise; reviewing a specialist workflow can also be demanding. The keypoint at line 66 then explicitly calls documentation “low-effort.” Replace the ordering sentence with: “Libraries can provide selected services directly and refer other needs to partners; the scope depends on local expertise and capacity.” Change “low-effort” to “familiar entry points, such as basic documentation guidance.” This avoids promising that such work is trivial.

The existing service-choice challenge meets the literal second objective, but repeats an answer learners have rehearsed in Episodes 3 and 4. Strengthen it within the same light case: “What would you help document first, and who would handle decisions about access?” Accept documentation review with referral for access questions, rather than treating every other service as wrong. Add one sentence of contrast: “A second researcher cannot recreate the required software setup: what service or partner would you involve?” Accept research computing or staff with environment-management expertise. This tests service triage without requiring the librarian to perform the analysis.

**6. Other substantive teaching issues**

**Overview, coin example, lines 62–76: a repeated non-significant conclusion is treated as reproducing a result, and failure to find a difference is described as support for fairness.** For novices this introduces a statistical misconception while distracting from the lesson objective. The strongest fix is to retain the familiar coin example but remove hypothesis testing: an analyst records 53 tails in 100 tosses and computes 53%; a second analyst reruns the same saved calculation on those observations and obtains 53%; a third collects 100 new tosses of the same coin to investigate the same question. Call that third study a replication attempt; its results determine whether it corroborates the first. Using another coin changes the target of the fairness claim. If retaining a test, explain that failure to detect a departure does not establish fairness, and compare actual outputs, not merely two “no difference” labels. The [American Statistical Association's explanation](https://www.amstat.org/news-listing/2021/10/08/editorial-calls-time-on-statistically-significant-in-research) supports this caution about treating a large p-value as evidence favoring a null hypothesis.

**Overview, qualitative process transparency, line 116: similar interpretation is presented as the general standard.** That collapses transparency into interpretive agreement. Replace with: “For this lesson, qualitative transparency means documenting how material was selected, coded and interpreted so another researcher can trace and critically assess the reasoning. They may reach a different interpretation.” This is consistent with [QDR's guide](https://qdr.syr.edu/ati/guide-ati), which emphasizes evidence, analytic explanation and differences among research traditions. In Tools 84, add the analytic explanation to Active Citation: the excerpt should be accompanied by why it supports the claim, not just a hyperlink. This does not reopen ATI's identity.

**Introduction 31 and 71; Overview 27, 48 and 199; Reference 8 and 14: the lesson sometimes overstates what reproduction demonstrates.** Depositing a package enables a check but does not establish that the package runs; reproducing a calculation does not establish that the study is accurate. A consistent mistake can reproduce. Rewrite the introduction example to say the successful colleague rerun demonstrates reproducibility. Add a sentence: “Matching results help us check a workflow; they do not by themselves show that its assumptions or conclusions are correct.” In the short definition, answer option and keypoint, include “same analysis steps” as well as “same data.” The [Turing Way definitions](https://book.the-turing-way.org/reproducible-research/overview/overview-definitions/) distinguish reproducing an analysis from testing its robustness with another analysis.

**Overview 35–39 and 88: the matrix and prose offer different shortcuts for generalization.** The prose emphasizes different contexts/populations; the matrix emphasizes different data/analysis. Add a caption explaining that the matrix is a simplified organizing aid: generalization concerns how far findings apply, not merely changing both inputs and analysis. Accept justified overlaps in scenario answers. The Turing Way itself cautions that changing a dataset and implementation does not by itself establish generalization.

**Overview 168–192; Instructor notes 34: crisis framing is improved, but the discussion lacks evidence for the judgment it demands.** The two citations now have useful descriptions, and the descriptions agree with [Fanelli's paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708272114) and [Munafò and colleagues' paper](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-022-05942-3). Both push back on “crisis” framing from different angles; they are not a simple pro/con pair. Supply two brief instructor summaries and ask: “What evidence would help you judge the extent of the problem? Which listed cause can a library service address?” Remove the request for an arbitrary successful-reproduction threshold. Keep disagreement welcome without requiring disagreement as the desired outcome. The current “fight the crisis” wording presupposes the frame learners were just asked to evaluate.

**Tools keypoints, line 141: renv versus containers is disproportionately prominent.** The distinction is correctly repaired and can remain in the body. Replace this keypoint with one aligned to the objective: “Use documentation to explain data and steps, and version control to inspect and recover changes.” That reinforces the learner performance rather than a prior review correction.

**Introduction, line 44: the forward reference is wrong.** Change “Episode 3 returns to this distinction with worked examples” to “Episode 2 develops this distinction through examples.” The current Episode 3 is about benefits and barriers.

**Instructor notes, line 29: optional compression can remove the only assessment of an objective.** It is reasonable to shorten Episode 3/5 case recaps, but preserve their service-choice/support checkpoints. State explicitly: “Compress the recap, not the challenge or its debrief.” Add the activity allocations above to the notes so a cold instructor has decisions to follow rather than only “budget extra time.”

The classification questions are not intrinsically bad closed-book trivia: short scenario choices are useful formative checks. The problems are the pre-instruction terminology in Episode 1, the ambiguous “same data” answer in Episode 2, and the absence of a reason in some responses. Requiring a brief justification addresses guessing without turning this into a programming lesson.

**Readiness judgment: 6/10 confidence that the lesson is ready for beta as currently written.** I would retain alpha until the assessment gaps, contradictory definitions and misleading statistical/qualitative explanations are corrected, first-use vocabulary is supported, and activity times are reconciled. Then run a cold-instructor pilot with the intended audience, recording activity durations and whether learners can distinguish openness from reproducibility, match tools to gaps, and choose a realistic service or referral. The case domain, light-spine scope and retained tool list do not need reopening. This score is a pedagogical judgment, not an official Carpentries lifecycle certification.

Corrections and qualifications to the earlier review:

- **Beta does not mean fully validated or stable.** CLDT defines beta as ready for instructors who were not significantly involved in development. It explicitly anticipates further beta pilots, with author briefing and debriefing. My earlier wording made a completed cold-instructor pilot sound like a prerequisite; that was too strict. Correct the key content/alignment problems, prepare the instructor handoff, and use that pilot as beta testing. A remaining minor wording issue need not delay beta. [Lifecycle definitions](https://carpentries.github.io/lesson-development-training/aio.html).
- **There is no mandated one-objective/one-challenge-block ratio.** The core requirement is alignment and usable evidence of learning. One discussion can check several objectives, and multiple-choice questions can diagnose misconceptions. The issue in the earlier table is what learners actually demonstrate, not block counts.
- **SMART does not require a separate deadline in every bullet.** The end of the lesson or episode supplies the timeframe. The 20–60-minute range and objective-count recommendation are planning guidance rather than automatic pass/fail tests.
- **My 55- and 50-minute proposals are estimates, not CLDT-prescribed durations.** The source supports measuring actual teaching time and reducing scope coherently when it does not fit. It does not establish the runtime of this particular revision.
- **A locally grounded case can still support broad reuse.** Explain cooling spaces and avoid assuming Los Angeles knowledge; there is no need to discard the agreed case domain.

Recommended order: reconcile the landing-page promises with the intended support skills; fix the content contradictions and assessment gaps; finish the instructor run sheet and pilot feedback plan; then invite an instructor outside the development team to teach the revised lesson as a beta pilot.
